Nerf Turret Wiring

One 4S pack feeds a common bus. Two ESCs spin the flywheels, three HW-216/DRV8825 boards drive pan, tilt and loader, and a buck converter powers the Raspberry Pi, which sends every control signal.

Three things the parts list doesn't cover

  1. A 5 V buck converter, 3 A or more. Nothing in the list turns 16.8 V into 5 V for the Pi. The Pi 4B wants 5.1 V at up to 3 A. Pick one whose input is rated well above 16.8 V and trim it to 5.1 V before connecting the Pi. Feeding pins 2/4 skips the Pi's input fuse; a USB-C pigtail from the buck is the safer route if you have one.
  2. A main fuse and a real disconnect. Two 40 A ESCs can pull far more than a hobby rocker switch handles; a 10 A switch will weld shut. Use an XT60 loop key (or an anti-spark switch) and a 30–40 A blade fuse on the + lead, right after the battery.
  3. Something watching cell voltage. No part in this design measures the pack. A cheap LiPo alarm on the balance lead stops you from over-discharging it.

Wire gauge: battery to bus 12–14 AWG, bus to each ESC 14 AWG, shared stepper trunk 20–22 AWG, branch to each VMOT, buck and motor coils 24 AWG, signals 24–26 AWG (Dupont is fine). Use stranded wire for anything that moves with the turret.

Raspberry Pi header

Pin map

STEP and DIR sit in GPIO 16–27, which default to pull-down at boot, so no driver sees a stray step while the Pi starts. EN sits on GPIO 4–6, which default to pull-up, so all three drivers stay disabled (motors unpowered) until your code drives EN low. GPIO 12/13 are the hardware-PWM pins. I²C, UART and SPI stay free for a camera, IMU or display later.

HW-216 boards × 3

Stepper driver setup

  • Motor power: the screw terminal (often marked 9–42 V or VMOT/GND) goes to the bus. 16.8 V is well inside the DRV8825's 8.2–45 V range.
  • Capacitor: one 100 µF 100 V cap per board, right at the motor-power terminal. Stripe = negative. Many HW-216 boards already carry one; adding yours in parallel does no harm.
  • Logic VCC: required. Connect Pi 3.3 V; the DRV8825 reads that as high. The microstep pins M0–M2 and RST/SLP are tied up to it.
  • RST and SLP: required. Both must be high or the DRV8825 stays asleep: no motor current and Vref reads 0 V. If the HW-216 brings them out as separate pins, jumper RST to SLP and both to VCC (3.3 V). The Pi must be powered while you set Vref, since VCC comes from it.
  • Coils: A1/A2 to one coil, B1/B2 to the other. Find the pairs with a multimeter (low resistance between wires of one coil). Never unplug a motor while the board is powered; that kills DRV8825s.
  • Current limit: set before the first run with VMOT on; the motor can be disconnected (Vref doesn't depend on it). Only plug or unplug the motor with VMOT off.

Microstep pins M0–M2 (on = wired to 3.3 V)

This HW-216 has no DIP switch: wire each "on" pin to VCC (3.3 V) and leave "off" pins open. The DRV8825 pulls open pins low internally, so with nothing connected it runs full-step. If a board does have a switch or jumpers, the same table applies; ignore any printed A4988 table.

ESCs × 2

Flywheel ESC setup

  • Power: red/black thick leads to the bus. Their own input caps are enough; a 100 µF across each is optional.
  • Servo lead: only signal and ground go to the Pi. If the lead has a red wire (BEC 5 V output), pull it out of the connector and tape it. Two BECs and a buck feeding the same 5 V rail fight each other.
  • Signal: 50 Hz servo pulses, 1000 µs = stop, 2000 µs = full. Generate them with pigpio (DMA-timed), not RPi.GPIO software PWM, which jitters and makes the motors stutter. Most ESCs accept 3.3 V signals; if one won't arm, add a 74AHCT125 level shifter.
  • Arming: send 1000 µs for 2–3 s after power-up. A full-throttle signal at power-on puts many ESCs into calibration mode.
  • Safety: at 4S, flywheels spin very fast. A second switch in the ESC + lead lets you power the Pi and steppers for testing while the flywheels stay dead.
Design trade-off

Can the Pi generate the step pulses fast enough?

Linux isn't real-time, and step timing is where that shows. A rough number for the loader at 1/8 step:

133 full steps/stroke × 8 = 1064 µsteps per stroke
2 strokes per dart × 3 darts/s ≈ 6400 steps/s on one axis, plus pan and tilt
  • Pi only (this diagram): works if steps come from pigpio waveforms, which are DMA-timed. A Python loop with time.sleep() will jitter and top out far below that rate, and that will look like the motor's limit when it isn't.
  • Pi + microcontroller: a Pi Pico or Arduino owns STEP/DIR/ESC timing; the Pi sends commands over UART. Costs one more board and a protocol, but timing is deterministic and a Pi crash or reboot can't leave a stepper mid-move. The STEP/DIR/EN wiring on the driver side stays identical, so you can switch later without rewiring the power side.
  • No endstops: every axis is open-loop. Nothing stops a move at the end of travel, and after skipped steps or a power cycle the position is unknown until you re-reference it by hand.
